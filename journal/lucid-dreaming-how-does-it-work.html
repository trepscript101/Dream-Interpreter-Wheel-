Lucid Dreaming: How Does It Work? What Happens in Your Brain

By Patrick Paul · October 10, 2026

I used to be amazed by lucid dreaming because it gives me the feeling that I am in charge and in control of my universe of dreams. The control you have is overwhelming when you suddenly become aware of yourself, actions and activities as the dream is taking place.

According to dictionary.com lucid dreaming is a dream in which a person is aware that they are dreaming and can often control action in the dream.

So the concept of lucid dreaming is becoming conscious when the dream is happening and you realize you have the power to control part or all aspects of the dream after becoming lucid.

In this article, we will surgically dissect and take a look at what lucid dreaming is, when it happens, what happens in the brain, and some of the methods people can use to experience as well as master it.

What Is Lucid Dreaming?

As we already know lucid dreaming is a dream where you simply become aware that you are in a dream.

Unlike in an ordinary dream,where you may actually have experience in strange places with people you may or may not know, in lucid dreams you can exert some level of control in who you are with and where you want to visit.

That moment of awareness is what makes the dream lucid as some people can fully control what happens after becoming aware. They may fly, change the location, talk to certain people, or create objects.

When Does Lucid Dreaming Happen?

According to Sleep foundation Lucid dreaming happens during REM (rapid eye movement) sleep, which is the stage of sleep where most vivid dreaming occurs.

Lucid dreams most often happen during REM sleep as REM cycles become longer and closer together as morning approaches, making lucid dreams much more likely to happen during the final hours of sleep

During Lucid Dreaming What Happens in The Brain?

In a lucid dream, the brain enters a hybrid state of consciousness, and cause a blending normal REM sleep with waking state self-awareness

However there are four key changes in the brain during lucid dreaming

During normal REM sleep, the part of the brain called the prefrontal cortex that aids clear thinking, planning, and knowing what is real becomes less active. During lucid dreaming however, some of these brain areas (dorsolateral, prefrontal and anterior prefrontal cortex) become active again. It's this function that makes a person realize that they are dreaming and sometimes control what happens in the dream.(https://lonestarneurology.net/others/how-lucid-dreaming-affects-brain-function-and-emotional-health/)

During a lucid dream, the areas of the brain known as the precuneus and parietal regions are active. These areas are responsible for making you understand who you are, what you are doing, and where you are. Because of increased activity you become aware that you are dreaming.

(https://www.medicalnewstoday.com/articles/326496)

In the REM sleep stage, the brain emits slower theta waves. On the other hand, in the lucid dream state, the brain may emit faster gamma, alpha, and beta waves. More common when we are awake and active. This explains why lucid dreams can feel more clear, real, and vivid.

Brain scans show that people who often have lucid dreams may have stronger connections between the frontal and parietal parts of the brain that help with thinking and understanding information. This stronger connection may allow the thinking part of the brain to communicate better with the part that creates dreams, helping people realize they are dreaming. (https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-018-36190-w)

Is The Brain Half Awake During a Lucid Dream?

No, the brain is not half awake during a lucid dream; rather, a person stays deeply asleep in REM (Rapid Eye Movement) sleep, however specific parts of the brain such as the prefrontal cortex and others linked to self-awareness and decision-making—temporarily reactivate while the person is enjoying deep sleep.

So in a lucid dream, a person is still deeply asleep. But the necessary parts of the brain are active, creating some form of awareness in the individual.

Lucid Dreaming Can It Be Learned?

Yes, if a person is consistent and interested in lucid dreaming they can through using some specific techniques

Some Lucid dreaming techniques

  • Dream Journaling: Most people start their dream recall journeys by having and writing in the journal every morning. This helps with training the brain to recognize patterns and develop the dream recall ability.
  • Reality Checks: Practice performing several reality checks and physical tests in the course of the day, for example practice the awareness of consciously checking a clock intermittently, breathe and be aware of actually breathing, looking at your hands and noticing different features and asking the yourself the question, "Am I dreaming?" All these build a habit that carries over into sleep.
  • MILD (Mnemonic Induction of Lucid Dreams): Set a clear intention as you drift off to sleep by repeating a phrase like, "The next time I dream, I will know that I am dreaming," use the power of visualization to imagine yourself becoming lucid in a dream.
  • WBTB (Wake-Back-to-Bed): Wake up after about 5 to 6 hours of sleep, stay awake for 20 to 30 minutes to increase alertness, and then return to bed to enter REM sleep with heightened awareness.
  • WILD (Wake-Induced Lucid Dreams): Lie still and maintain mental consciousness as your body transitions directly from a waking state into a REM sleep dream state.

While some people naturally experience lucid dreams. Others have tried to use the different techniques above to increase their chances of having it.

Can You Control a Lucid Dream?

Yes, I believe certain aspects of a lucid dream can be controlled. When you become lucid you can control how you act, what you say and who you are with and where you want to go. You however have little control over shaping or changing some environmental features.

There's still some element in the subconscious mind that can still impose limitations on the lucid dreamer such as resisting some control and waking up the dreamer against his will when they get overly excited or fearful.

Are Lucid Dreams Safe?

Trying to lucid dream can be stressful not necessarily dangerous. This is because the methods employed to induce lucid dreaming can make regular and normal sleep strenuous.

For example, practicing WBTB (wake-back-to-bed) repeatedly in the course of the night may cause poor sleep or daytime drowsiness.

Sleep paralysis can happen when a person is suffering from insufficient sleep. Also, a lack of sleep can cause other health related issues.

It's better to focus on healthy sleep rather than trying to force lucid dreams every night.

Can Lucid Dreaming Help With Nightmares?

Yes, Lucid dreaming may help limit the intensity of how often nightmares occur, and the associated stress involved through what is known as Lucid Dreaming Therapy (LDT).

How It Helps With Nightmares

  • Realizing you are dreaming: When you become aware of the dream during a nightmare, the fear may dissipate because you know that you are safely in bed.
  • Changing the dream: When you are aware that you are in a dream, you can change what happens. You could turn a mighty monster into something docile, fly away up into the sky from danger, and turn an event into a happier one.
  • Facing the fear: Some people choose to face the scary person or thing in their dream. They may talk to it, ask why it is there, or find a way to make it less frightening.
  • Processing trauma: Researchers believe that when you work with dreams, it may help people cope with painful or frightening experiences especially with people who have trauma-related nightmares, including those linked to PTSD.

However, lucid dreaming should not be used in lieu for mental health treatment
