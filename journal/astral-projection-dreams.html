Astral Projection and Out-of-Body Dreams

This one overlaps a lot with sleep paralysis, which I wrote about separately — but it deserves its own look, because people describe it so differently. Sleep paralysis is almost always described with fear. Astral projection is usually described the opposite way: calm, deliberate, sometimes even sought out on purpose.

The sensation itself is specific: a feeling of floating above or outside your own body, sometimes watching yourself from a distance, sometimes drifting through the room or beyond it. Whatever you make of the experience, the sensation is real and it's been genuinely studied — not just anecdotally reported.

What the research actually found

Neuroscientist Olaf Blanke ran a series of experiments in the 2000s that got real attention: by electrically stimulating a specific brain region called the temporo-parietal junction — the area responsible for integrating your sense of body position in space — he was able to reliably trigger out-of-body sensations in patients, on demand, in a lab. When that region misfires or gets disrupted, the brain's sense of "where I am" briefly disconnects from the body it's actually in.

During the transition into sleep, that same region is doing a lot of active recalibrating as the body relaxes and sensory input drops off. A brief misfire during that transition — a common and largely harmless glitch — can produce the exact floating, outside-the-body sensation people describe as astral projection.

Why it feels so deliberate

Unlike a typical dream, out-of-body experiences often happen right at the edge of sleep, in that thin, half-aware window before full unconsciousness — which is likely why they feel more directed and less chaotic than an ordinary dream. You're closer to waking thought than deep dreaming, so the experience carries more of the structure and apparent intention of a waking mind.
