The Titanic Dream Phenomenon

This is the story that got me genuinely interested in prophetic dreams in the first place — not a personal experience, but a historical one. In the years and decades after the Titanic sank in 1912, dozens of people came forward claiming they'd dreamed warnings before the ship ever left port. Some said they cancelled their tickets because of it.

Fourteen years earlier, in 1898, a novelist named Morgan Robertson published a book called Futility, about a massive "unsinkable" ocean liner called the Titan that strikes an iceberg and sinks in the North Atlantic. The similarities to the real disaster — the name, the size, the "unsinkable" claim, even rough passenger numbers — are close enough that it's been retold as an eerie premonition for over a century.

A real research project, not just folklore

What's less well known is that disaster premonitions actually got formally studied. In 1966, following a landslide disaster in the Welsh village of Aberfan that killed over a hundred people, a British psychiatrist named John Barker collected reports from people who claimed to have dreamed about the disaster beforehand. He went on to found something called the Premonitions Bureau, actively collecting dream reports from the public in the hope of catching a genuine prediction before a disaster happened, rather than after.

The project ran for years and collected thousands of submissions. What it found, ultimately, wasn't evidence of prophecy — it was an enormous volume of ordinary anxiety dreams, the vast majority of which matched nothing at all. The few that seemed to "hit" were the ones people remembered and reported after the fact, out of thousands that never got a second thought.

What's actually going on

Robertson himself, when asked about his novel decades later, pointed to his own genuine expertise as a former sailor with real knowledge of ship design and North Atlantic shipping routes — an informed guess dressed up by hindsight as prophecy. The Titanic dreams work the same way: with a major, genuinely newsworthy disaster, retrospective reporting bias does enormous work. After a tragedy makes headlines, people who had any anxious, ship-related, or vaguely ominous dream around that time are far more likely to reinterpret it as a premonition, and far more likely to actually mention it to someone, than they would be on an ordinary day.
